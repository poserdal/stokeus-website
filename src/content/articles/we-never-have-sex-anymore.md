## Why did sex just stop in our relationship?

You didn't decide to stop. It just happened. Once a week turned into once every two weeks, then once a month, then it's been so long you've stopped counting. Now you're here, googling "we never have sex anymore" at 11 p.m., wondering if everyone else's relationship is secretly like this too.

They are. This is one of the most common things that happens in long-term relationships. It's embarrassing. It feels like failure. And most advice makes it worse by telling you to communicate better, as if you just forgot how to talk to each other.

### What's the real problem when sex stops?

Every article says the same thing. Schedule intimacy. Go on date nights. Light some candles. That stuff works if you're bored. It doesn't work if desire itself has gone missing.

Here's what actually happens. Somewhere in the middle of jobs and kids and trying to remember if you paid the electric bill, you both stopped being people your partner wanted to have sex with. Not because you got less attractive. Because you became completely known. Predictable. Safe.

Desire needs a little bit of distance. A little bit of mystery. When your entire life is shared logistics and who's picking up dinner, there's nothing left to want.

> Desire doesn't die because you're too familiar. It dies because you stopped existing as a person outside the relationship.

### Should I force myself to want sex?

The advice to "just initiate" is garbage. You can't force desire. You can't decide to want sex the way you decide to go to the gym. Trying to have sex you don't actually want makes the problem worse, not better.

What you can do is remove the things that killed desire. Resentment. Exhaustion. The feeling that your partner only touches you when they want something. Those things don't go away because you scheduled Thursday night. They go away when you rebuild the emotional safety that makes vulnerability possible again.

### How do I talk about our lack of sex without making it worse?

At some point, someone has to say it out loud. Not "why don't you want me anymore." Not "we need to have sex more." Both of those are accusations pretending to be questions.

Try this instead. Pick a calm moment. Not after a rejection. Not at midnight. Say something like "I miss feeling close to you, and I don't know how we got here." That's not blame. That's honesty.

The conversation is not going to fix it. The point is to agree that it matters and that you're both willing to try.

### Should I start with non-sexual touch?

If it's been months, jumping straight back to sex is like running a marathon after sitting on the couch for a year. Your body doesn't trust it. Neither does your partner's.

Start smaller. A hand on their shoulder when you walk by. Sitting close enough that your legs touch. A hug that lasts more than two seconds. Touch that says "I like you" instead of "I want something from you."

Do that for a week. Then another week. Let your body remember that touch doesn't always lead somewhere.

### Should I work on myself first?

Most people in a sexless relationship have let themselves disappear. You used to have hobbies. Opinions. A life that wasn't entirely about managing the household and keeping everyone else happy.

Your partner fell in love with a person. Right now, you might be more of a role. Parent. Coworker. Roommate. Go do something for yourself. Not for the relationship. For you. Take the class. Meet the friend. Spend a Saturday doing the thing you keep saying you'll get back to. Desire comes back when there's a person worth desiring again.

### How long should I wait for things to change?

This doesn't fix in a week. You're going to try things that don't work. Have conversations that go badly.

But if you're trying and your partner isn't, if months go by and nothing changes, that's real information. Some relationships end not because anyone did anything wrong, but because one person stopped choosing it. You get to decide how long you're willing to wait for someone to choose you back.
