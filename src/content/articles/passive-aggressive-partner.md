## How do I deal with a passive aggressive partner?

You know what passive aggression looks like. The silent treatment that lasts for hours. The exaggerated sigh when you ask a simple question. The 'nothing's wrong' delivered in a tone that makes it very clear something is extremely wrong.

You're not imagining it. And you're not overreacting. Living with a passive aggressive partner is exhausting because you're constantly translating. They won't tell you what they're upset about, but they'll punish you for it anyway.

The worst part? When you bring it up, they can deny everything. That's the whole design of passive aggression. It's anger with plausible deniability.

### Should I call out passive aggression?

The first move is to stop pretending you don't notice. Passive aggression survives in the gray area where nobody acknowledges what's happening. Kill the gray area.

Next time your partner does the thing, say it plainly. Not as an accusation. Just as an observation. "You seem upset but you're saying you're fine. I'd rather know what's actually going on."

You're not asking them to admit they're being passive aggressive. You're just refusing to play along with the fiction that everything is normal. That alone changes the dynamic.

> Passive aggression only works if you pretend not to notice. Stop pretending.

### Why do I keep trying to guess what's wrong?

This is the trap. Your partner won't tell you what's wrong, so you start running through every possible thing you might have done. You apologize for three different things just to cover your bases. And in doing that, you've rewarded the passive aggression.

They got your attention. They got you scrambling. And they never had to say a single direct word about what they actually needed.

Break the pattern. When they shut down or go cold, don't chase. Don't guess. Just say, "I can tell something's off. When you're ready to talk about it, I'm here." Then stop talking.

It feels counterintuitive. But you're not being cold. You're refusing to participate in a game where one person withholds information and the other person does all the emotional labor of figuring it out.

### How do I get my partner to be more direct?

Passive aggression happens for a reason. Usually because somewhere along the line, your partner learned that being direct leads to conflict, dismissal, or punishment. So they went underground.

If you want them to stop, you have to make honesty safer than hinting. That means when they do say something directly, even if it's hard to hear, you don't punish them for it. You thank them.

"I'm frustrated that you didn't do the dishes again." That's direct. It might sting. But your response should be, "Thanks for telling me. I hear you." Not defensiveness. Not a list of reasons why you didn't do them. Just acknowledgment.

Do that enough times and the cost-benefit analysis of passive aggression starts to shift. Direct becomes easier. Indirect becomes less appealing.

### How much passive aggression should I tolerate?

Here's the thing nobody says. You can do everything right and your partner might not change. Passive aggression is a deeply ingrained pattern. Some people have been doing it since childhood. They might not even realize they're doing it.

You can't control whether they change. You can only control what you're willing to live with. If the silent treatment lasts three days every time there's a minor conflict, that's not sustainable. If sarcasm is their primary love language, that's corrosive.

At some point, you have to decide. Is this a relationship where two people are working on the same problem? Or is this a relationship where you're the only one doing the work?

That's not a threat. It's not an ultimatum. It's just clarity. Some patterns are fixable. Some aren't. Knowing the difference is not giving up. It's being honest about what kind of relationship you're actually in.
