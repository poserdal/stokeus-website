## What's the difference between a complaint and criticism?

A complaint is about a specific thing that happened. "You forgot to call the plumber and now I have to deal with it" is a complaint. It's direct, it's about a behavior, and it gives your partner something they can actually respond to.

Criticism is about their character. "You never follow through on anything" is criticism. You're not addressing the plumber. You're telling them what kind of person they are. And once you make it about who they are, they can't fix it without becoming someone else.

Gottman found that criticism is one of the four patterns that predict divorce. Not because complaints are bad. Because complaints that turn into character attacks stop being solvable.

### Why does criticism feel so natural when I'm frustrated?

Because you're not just frustrated about this one thing. You're frustrated about a pattern. The plumber isn't the real issue. The real issue is that you've been here before. You feel like you're always picking up the slack. So instead of addressing the plumber, you address everything at once.

That's how complaints turn into criticism. The current incident becomes evidence in a larger case you've been building. And once you start presenting that case, your partner stops hearing the specific problem and starts defending their whole identity.

> Criticism is a complaint with resentment attached. That's why it lands so differently.

### How do I complain without criticizing?

Stay specific. Name the thing that happened. Name how it affected you. Stop before you make it about their character.

Instead of "you never help with the kids," try "I needed help with bedtime tonight and I didn't get it. I'm exhausted."

Instead of "you're always on your phone," try "I've been trying to talk to you and you've been scrolling. Can you put it down for a minute?"

The complaint gives them something to respond to. The criticism gives them something to defend against. Those are different conversations.

### What if the same thing keeps happening?

That's when it gets hard. If you've complained about the same thing five times and nothing changed, the temptation to escalate is real. "I've told you this before and you still don't care" is technically a complaint, but it sounds like criticism because of the accumulated frustration behind it.

Here's what works better: acknowledge the pattern without making it an indictment. "This has come up before and I don't know what to do anymore. Can we actually talk about why it keeps happening?"

That opens a door instead of building a wall.

### What do I do when my partner criticizes me?

If you're on the receiving end of criticism, your instinct will be to defend yourself. That usually makes things worse.

Try this instead: "I hear you're frustrated. What specifically happened that bothered you?" You're asking them to turn the criticism into a complaint. Give them a chance to be specific about the thing that actually upset them instead of the narrative they've built around it.

It won't always work. But it's better than meeting criticism with defensiveness and watching the conversation spiral.

### Is the goal to never criticize?

The goal is to stay close enough to your partner that the frustration doesn't pile up. Criticism usually isn't about the current incident. It's about the resentment you've been swallowing.

Deal with the small things when they're small. Complain about tonight's dishes tonight. Not next month when you've catalogued every dish left in the sink for weeks. The more current you keep your complaints, the less likely they are to turn into criticism.
