## Why do some couples make it and others don't?

Gottman spent decades watching couples in his research lab. Measuring heart rates. Coding facial expressions. Tracking who stayed together and who didn't. He found a lot of patterns. But one stood out above the rest.

Couples who stayed married turned toward each other 86% of the time. Couples who divorced averaged 33%.

That's it. That's the difference between couples who make it and couples who don't. Not how much they fought. Not how compatible they were on paper. Whether they responded when their partner reached for them.

### What does "turning toward" actually mean?

Turning toward means responding when your partner makes a bid for connection. A bid is any attempt to get your attention, affection, or engagement. It could be a question, a comment, a touch, or just a look.

When your partner says "look at this sunset" and you look up, that's turning toward. When they reach for your hand and you squeeze back, that's turning toward. When they mention something from their day and you ask a follow-up question, that's turning toward.

It's not about grand gestures. It's about the micro-moments. The tiny acknowledgments that say I see you. I'm here. You matter.

> The difference between couples who make it and couples who don't is measured in seconds, not hours.

### Why do couples turn away from each other?

Nobody turns away on purpose. Not usually. You turn away because you're tired. Because you're on your phone. Because your brain is somewhere else. Because their comment didn't seem important enough to warrant your full attention.

The problem is that it adds up. One missed bid doesn't matter. A hundred of them over a month does. Your partner stops reaching because it doesn't work. You stop noticing because they stopped reaching. And then one day you're sitting in the same room feeling like strangers.

Turning away isn't malicious. It's just careless. And carelessness, compounded over years, kills relationships as surely as betrayal does.

### How do I start turning toward more often?

You don't need to be perfectly present all the time. You just need to catch more bids than you miss.

Start by noticing when your partner is reaching. Not just the obvious moments. The small ones. The comment that seems random. The sigh. The way they look at you when they walk in the room. Those are all bids.

Then respond. Even briefly. Even if you're busy. A "yeah?" or a quick glance or a touch on their shoulder. You're not solving anything. You're just acknowledging that they exist and that you noticed.

### Can you recover if you've been turning away?

Yes. The couples who recovered weren't the ones who never turned away. They were the ones who noticed the pattern and changed it.

Start tonight. When your partner says something, look at them. When they reach for you, reach back. Not because you feel like it. Because the research says this is what keeps people together.

It's not romantic. It's not exciting. It's the boring, repeated choice to notice your partner when it would be easier not to. That's what the 86% is made of.
