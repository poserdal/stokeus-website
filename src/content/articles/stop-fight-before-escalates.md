## Why do small disagreements turn into big fights?

Because you're not actually fighting about what you think you're fighting about. The dishes aren't the real issue. The fact that you feel unseen, or taken for granted, or like you're always the one handling everything while they coast, that's the issue. And all that context comes flooding in the moment they leave a dish in the sink.

Small triggers access big files. The argument escalates because the underlying frustration has been building. And now one dirty dish has to carry the weight of six months of unspoken resentment.

### How do I know when a fight is about to escalate?

Your body knows before your brain does. Heart rate goes up. You feel defensive. You stop trying to understand and start trying to win. Your responses get shorter and sharper. You start using words like "always" and "never."

The moment you hear yourself saying "you always" or "you never," you're no longer in a productive conversation. You're in a fight. And it's about to get worse.

> Arguments escalate when you stop trying to be heard and start trying to hurt.

### What do I do in that moment?

Call a timeout. Not passive aggressively. Not "whatever, I'm done." Actually say: "I feel this getting bigger than it needs to be. I want to pause before we say something we can't take back. Can we come back to this in 20 minutes?"

That's a repair attempt. You're acknowledging the spiral and choosing to exit it. The key is to actually come back. A timeout isn't an escape hatch. It's a pause.

### Why does taking a break feel like losing?

Because you've been trained to think that walking away means you're weak. Or that they'll think you're conceding. Or that the issue won't get resolved if you don't push through right now.

None of that is true. Pushing through while flooded guarantees you'll say something damaging. Taking a break lets your nervous system calm down so you can actually have a productive conversation later. It's the smarter move. Not the weaker one.

### What do I do during the timeout?

Not stew. Not rehearse your argument. Actually calm down. Go for a walk. Do something that shifts your state. The goal is to get your heart rate below 100 bpm so you can think clearly again.

If you spend the timeout building your case, you'll come back just as activated. The point is to reset, not reload.

### How do I come back after a timeout?

Gently. Start with connection, not content. "I didn't want us to spiral. I care about this and I want to figure it out together." That sets a different tone than jumping right back into the disagreement.

Then try to identify what the fight was actually about. Usually it's not the surface issue. There's a feeling underneath that needs to be named. "I think I was really reacting to feeling like my effort isn't noticed." That gives your partner something to respond to that isn't an attack.
