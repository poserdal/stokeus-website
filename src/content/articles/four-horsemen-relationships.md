## What are the four horsemen that predict divorce?

Gottman watched thousands of couples fight. He measured their heart rates. Coded their facial expressions. Tracked who stayed together and who split.

He found four patterns that predicted divorce with over 90% accuracy. He called them the four horsemen: criticism, contempt, defensiveness, and stonewalling.

Every couple does some of these sometimes. The question is whether they've become your default. Because when these patterns are how you fight, you're not solving problems. You're eroding the relationship with every conversation.

### What's the difference between criticism and a complaint?

A complaint is about a specific behavior. "You didn't take out the trash and I'm frustrated." That's fair. That's something to work with.

Criticism is about their character. "You never take out the trash because you don't care about this house." That's an attack. You're not addressing the trash. You're telling them what kind of person they are.

Criticism starts with "you always" or "you never" and usually includes a judgment about who they are, not just what they did. The fix is simple: stick to the specific thing that happened. Not their entire personality.

### Why is contempt worse than yelling?

Contempt is the number one predictor of divorce. Not infidelity. Not fighting. Contempt.

It's the eye-roll. The sarcastic "sure, whatever." The mocking tone when you repeat what they just said. It's communicating that your partner is beneath you. Not just wrong. Worthless.

You can recover from a fight. You can even recover from a screaming match. But contempt tells your partner that you don't respect them anymore. And relationships cannot survive without respect.

> Contempt isn't just how you fight. It's how you've started to see them. That's why it's lethal.

### Why do I get so defensive with my partner?

Defensiveness is when your partner says something and you immediately explain why you're not wrong. You deflect blame. You counter-attack. You make it about what they did instead of what you did.

It feels like self-protection. But it tells your partner that you're not safe to talk to. That bringing up a problem with you will turn into a trial where they have to prove their case beyond a reasonable doubt.

The antidote is painful but effective: take some responsibility. Even a little. "You're right, I should have texted you" shuts down the spiral faster than any defense ever could.

### What do I do if my partner stonewalls?

Stonewalling is when someone shuts down completely. Goes silent. Leaves the room. Refuses to engage. It looks like they don't care. But most of the time, they're flooded. Their nervous system is overwhelmed and they literally can't process what you're saying.

The fix isn't pushing harder. It's giving them space to regulate, with a commitment to come back. "Let's take twenty minutes and then try again." Not as punishment. As a genuine pause.

### Can we fix these patterns?

Yes. But you have to do more than recognize them. You have to practice the antidotes until they become automatic.

Instead of criticism: complaint. Instead of contempt: appreciation. Instead of defensiveness: responsibility. Instead of stonewalling: self-soothing and re-engaging.

It's not complicated. It's just hard to remember when you're already upset. The couples who beat the four horsemen aren't the ones who know about them. They're the ones who've practiced the alternatives until they don't have to think about it anymore.
